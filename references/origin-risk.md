# Origin risk — the ground, not just the company

A legitimacy check answers *is this firm real*. It does not answer *what is in the soil where
this grew*. These are separate questions and a clean answer to the first says nothing about the
second. A company can be a hundred years old, publicly listed, scandal-free, and still be
farming a floodplain downstream of a smelter.

Run this whenever the product is an agricultural commodity that concentrates soil contaminants:
spices, herbs, tea, rice, cocoa, root vegetables, leafy greens, seaweed, mushrooms.

## Step 1 — Locate the growing region, not the head office

The company address is where the paperwork lives. Find where the crop is actually grown, which
is often a different province or country. Look for a named planting base, a sourcing region, a
DOP/PGI designation, or trade reporting. Record the resolution you achieved: country, province,
or prefecture. **Resolution matters** — a provincial average can hide a clean district and a
failing one.

## Step 2 — Ask what is known about that ground

Search for the region plus: soil heavy metals, mining, smelting, industrial history, geological
background.

Distinguish two causes, because they behave differently and neither is automatically worse:

- **Industrial contamination** — mining, smelting, coal combustion, e-waste. Usually localised,
  sometimes severe, often with a documented history and a cleanup record.
- **High geological background** — the underlying rock carries metals before anyone adds any.
  Karst and carbonate terrain, volcanic soils, serpentine. Not anyone's fault, not remediable,
  and it still ends up in the crop.

Report which one you found. "The region is a historic mining district" and "the region's bedrock
is naturally metal-rich" are different sentences with different implications for whether
anything can be fixed.

## Step 3 — Find a measurement of the commodity itself

**This is the step that matters most and the one most likely to be skipped.** Regional soil data
is a proxy. What you want is somebody who measured the actual food.

Search: `<commodity> <region> heavy metals` / `<commodity> <region> lead cadmium arsenic study`
and look for peer-reviewed work, national monitoring programmes, or consumer-organisation
testing. Prefer papers that report sample count, sampling year, and a health-risk assessment.

Then take the numbers to `contaminant-thresholds.md` and report them against every threshold
that applies, not just the one that supports the answer you already have.

If nobody has measured it, say so plainly. An unmeasured commodity is an open question, not a
clean result.

## Step 4 — Check the sample frame before borrowing a study

**A study only speaks about what it sampled.** This is the easiest way to launder a real finding
into a false claim, and it is easy to do by accident.

Before citing a study against the product in front of you, confirm:

- **Market**: were the samples bought in the market this product is sold in?
- **Origin**: does the study report countries of origin, or only retail brands?
- **Date**: how old is the sampling, and has the supply chain changed since?
- **Commodity match**: same crop, same form (fresh, dried, ground)?

Worked failure, from the session that produced this file: a Consumer Reports study of
**126 herbs and spices from 38 US retail brands**, with **no countries of origin published**,
was almost used to cast doubt on a jar of oregano posted from Italy. It is a finding about the
American spice aisle. The correct move was to go and find European testing of European oregano
instead — which existed, and said something sharper.

When the sample frame does not match, **say the study cannot speak to this product** and go
looking for one that can. Do not soften it into "studies suggest."

## Step 5 — What not to do

- **Never infer disease from geography.** Elevated regional cancer or illness statistics have
  their own attributed causes — smoking, poverty, healthcare access, occupational exposure —
  and attributing them to a food product without a study that makes that link is defamation
  dressed as caution. Report what the epidemiology actually attributes.
- **Never let "the company is huge and legitimate" answer an origin question.** It is the wrong
  evidence for that question. Note the mismatch explicitly.
- **Never treat a clean regional average as clearance** for a product from an unidentified
  district within that region.
